The whole idea in sixty seconds

A blockchain is a shared record book that thousands of strangers can each hold a copy of, and that nobody can quietly rewrite. New entries are bundled into blocks. Each block carries the cryptographic fingerprint (hash) of the block before it, so changing any old entry changes every fingerprint after it and the tampering is obvious. The strangers agree on which new block to add next using a consensus mechanism that makes cheating expensive and honesty profitable. Add digital signatures (so only the owner of a coin can spend it) and you get money that needs no bank. Add a programmable layer and you get smart contracts: small programs whose execution everyone can verify.

Part I

Foundations

What a blockchain is, why anyone invented one, and the building blocks every chain shares.

1. What is a blockchain?

⏱ 5 min Beginner

A definition you can hold in your head

A blockchain is an append-only list of records, grouped into blocks, where each block contains a cryptographic fingerprint of the previous block. The list is copied across many computers (nodes) that follow the same rules and use a consensus mechanism to agree on which block comes next. Because every fingerprint depends on everything before it, you cannot change an old record without breaking all the links that follow, and everyone holding a copy would notice.

An analogy: imagine a village ledger kept in a notebook. Every page begins with a short code computed from the previous page's entire contents. If someone scribbles over page 12, the code at the top of page 13 no longer matches, and so does every page after it. Now imagine thousands of villagers each keep an identical notebook and a rule that a new page is only accepted if most of them accept it. To rewrite history you would have to forge the pages and out-vote or out-work everyone else. That, in one paragraph, is a blockchain.

Four properties to remember
  1. Shared. Many independent nodes hold the same data. There is no master copy.
  2. Append-only. You add new blocks; you do not edit or delete old ones.
  3. Tamper-evident. Hash links make any alteration visible to every node.
  4. Agreed. A consensus protocol decides the single official order of entries.

Many chains add a fifth: programmable. They can run code (smart contracts) whose results every node can recompute and verify.

Three layers plus a layer of incentives

It helps to see a blockchain as a stack. Each layer solves a different problem, and the layers are separable, which is why so many different chains exist.

This is the comparison that matters for judging whether a blockchain is the right tool. A normal database run by one organisation is faster, cheaper, private and easy to fix. A blockchain gives up all of that to buy one thing: agreement between parties who do not trust each other, with no referee.

What "immutable" really means

Blockchains are often called immutable. More precisely they are tamper-evident and economically hard to rewrite. Three honest caveats:

  • Rewriting is possible but costly. A sufficiently powerful attacker can rewrite recent blocks. The deeper a block is buried, the more work or stake is needed to undo it (chapter 17 quantifies this).
  • The social layer can override the code. In 2016 the Ethereum community chose to fork the chain to reverse the effects of the DAO exploit. Bitcoin's community has refused every such request. Both are choices made by people.
  • Garbage in, garbage forever. A chain guarantees that a record was written, not that it is true. A forged invoice hashed onto a blockchain is a permanent, perfectly preserved forgery.
Common misconceptions
  • "Blockchain means Bitcoin." Bitcoin is one application of the idea.
  • "Blockchain data is encrypted." On public chains it is signed, not encrypted. Anyone can read it.
  • "Transactions are anonymous." They are pseudonymous: tied to addresses, which can often be linked to real people (chapter 18).
  • "A blockchain is always decentralised." Decentralisation is a spectrum you have to measure: who runs nodes, who writes the software, who holds most of the stake or hash power?
Check yourself

Q1. Name the four properties that define a blockchain. Q2. Why is a database usually the better choice when a single company controls all writes?

Answers. (1) Shared, append-only, tamper-evident, agreed by consensus. (2) With one trusted writer there is nobody to agree with, so the cost of consensus buys nothing; a database is faster, cheaper and can fix mistakes.

2. The problem it solves

⏱ 4 min Beginner

Digital things are infinitely copyable

A photo can be copied a million times with no loss. That is wonderful for photos and fatal for money. If a coin is just a file, you can spend it, keep the original, and spend it again. This is the double-spend problem. For decades the only fix was a trusted third party: a bank or payment company that keeps the master ledger and refuses the second spend.

That arrangement works, but it has structural costs that motivated Bitcoin's design:

  • Fees and friction. Intermediaries charge for resolving disputes, so small payments and cross-border transfers are expensive or slow.
  • Reversibility. Because disputes are mediated, transactions can be reversed, so merchants demand extra personal information to manage fraud risk.
  • Single point of failure and control. The ledger holder can be hacked, make mistakes, freeze accounts, or be pressured by governments.
  • Silos. Every institution keeps its own books, so reconciling them takes days and entire back-office departments.

The deeper problem: agreement without a boss

Remove the trusted ledger-keeper and a computer-science problem appears. Many machines, some unreliable or dishonest, must agree on one history of events, over a network where messages can be delayed, lost or forged. This is the distributed consensus problem, and computer scientists had studied it for decades.

The Byzantine Generals

In 1982 Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease described the Byzantine Generals Problem. Several generals surround a city and must all attack or all retreat. They communicate only by messengers, and some generals may be traitors who send conflicting messages. Can the loyal generals reach agreement anyway?

Key results that shape every blockchain design:

  • With classical protocols, you can tolerate f traitors only if there are at least 3f + 1 participants. Roughly, a third of the voters can be malicious, no more. Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT, Castro and Liskov, 1999) made this efficient enough to use, but it needs a fixed, known list of participants.
  • The FLP result (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson, 1985) proves that in a fully asynchronous network no deterministic protocol can guarantee agreement if even one node may crash. Real systems get around it with timeouts, randomness or assumptions about timing.
  • The CAP theorem says that during a network partition a distributed system must choose between consistency and availability. Blockchains generally keep availability and settle consistency later (an eventual, probabilistic agreement).

The Sybil problem

Voting protocols count participants, so in an open network an attacker simply creates a million fake identities and out-votes everyone. This is the Sybil attack (named by John Douceur, 2002). Any permissionless system needs identities that are costly to create. Bitcoin makes the cost electricity and hardware (proof of work). Proof-of-stake chains make it locked-up capital.

Nakamoto's insight

Bitcoin's creator combined existing ideas into something new. Rather than trying to be certain about agreement, the protocol makes agreement probabilistic but exponentially reliable, and ties voting power to a scarce physical resource instead of to identities:

  1. Transactions are broadcast to everyone, with no central coordinator.
  2. Anyone may try to package recent transactions into a block by solving a hard hash puzzle (proof of work). Solving it is expensive; checking the solution is nearly free.
  3. Nodes always treat the chain with the most accumulated work as the truth, so the "vote" is one-CPU-one-vote, not one-IP-one-vote.
  4. The winner of each puzzle earns newly created coins plus fees, which makes following the rules the most profitable strategy for almost everyone.
  5. Each new block built on top of yours makes reversing your transaction exponentially less likely.
Why order matters

Double-spending is really an ordering problem. If everyone agrees that payment A came before payment B, then B (spending the same coin) is simply invalid. A blockchain is a machine that produces one globally agreed order of transactions without a central clock. Everything else follows from that.

Trust, relocated rather than removed

"Trustless" is a slogan, not a fact. You still trust: the cryptography is sound, the software has no fatal bug, a majority of work or stake is not colluding, and that the community will keep following the rules you signed up for. What changes is where trust sits: from a named institution to open-source code and economic incentives that anyone can inspect. Whether that is better depends on the use case, which is the subject of chapter 19.

Check yourself

Q1. Why is "one IP address, one vote" unusable in an open network? Q2. What is the maximum fraction of Byzantine participants a classical BFT protocol can tolerate?

Answers. (1) Sybil attacks: identities are free to fake. (2) Fewer than one third (n ≥ 3f + 1).

3. Cryptographic building blocks

⏱ 7 min Beginner

Four pieces of cryptography carry almost the entire weight of a blockchain: hash functions, hash-linked structures (the chain and Merkle trees), digital signatures, and the proof-of-work puzzle built from hashing. Learn these and the rest is bookkeeping.

3.1 Hash functions

A cryptographic hash function takes any input (a word, a movie, a block) and returns a short fixed-size fingerprint called a digest. Bitcoin uses SHA-256, which always outputs 256 bits (64 hex characters). Ethereum uses Keccak-256. Bitcoin's address scheme also uses RIPEMD-160 to shorten a public-key hash.

A hash function earns the word "cryptographic" with these properties:

PropertyPlain meaningWhy a blockchain needs it

Pre-image resistantGiven a digest you cannot find an input that produces it, except by guessingThis is why mining is a lottery of guesses
Second-pre-image resistantGiven one input you cannot find a different one with the same digestPrevents swapping a transaction for a forged one
Collision resistantYou cannot find any two inputs with the same digestKeeps fingerprints unique (about 2128 work to find a SHA-256 collision by brute force)
Avalanche effectChange one bit of input and about half the output bits flipA tiny tamper produces a completely different, unpredictable digest

Bitcoin applies SHA-256 twice (written sha256d) to block headers and transactions, mainly as a defensive habit against length-extension attacks. You will run both in chapter 11.

3.2 Hash pointers: how blocks are chained

The "chain" in blockchain is just each block storing the hash of the previous block's header. That hash commits to everything in the previous block, including the hash it stored of the block before that, and so on back to the very first block (the genesis block).

3.3 Merkle trees: one hash for thousands of transactions

A block may contain thousands of transactions. Instead of hashing them as one huge blob, they are arranged in a Merkle tree (Ralph Merkle, 1979): hash every transaction, hash the hashes in pairs, repeat until a single Merkle root remains. Only the root goes into the block header.

Why this matters

Merkle proofs let a phone verify that a payment is in a block by downloading an 80-byte header and a few hashes, not gigabytes of data. This is how light clients (SPV, "simplified payment verification") work. Ethereum uses a related structure, the Merkle-Patricia trie, to commit to its entire account state.

Merkle tree builder

One transaction per line (up to 16). Edit a line: the amber nodes show exactly which hashes change, from the leaf up to the root. Hover a node for its full hash.

3.4 Digital signatures: proving ownership without revealing a secret

Blockchains have no usernames or passwords. Ownership is proved with public-key cryptography.

  • You generate a random 256-bit number: your private key. Keep it secret.
  • Multiplying a fixed point on an elliptic curve by that number yields your public key. The reverse step (the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem) is believed infeasible.
  • To spend, you sign the transaction with the private key. Anyone can verify the signature against your public key. It proves "the owner of this key authorised exactly this message" without revealing the key.
  • Change one bit of the transaction and the signature fails to verify.
SchemeUsed byNotes
ECDSA on secp256k1Bitcoin, Ethereum accountsThe classic. Needs a fresh secret random value for every signature or the private key leaks.
Schnorr (BIP 340)Bitcoin Taproot (2021)Simpler maths, supports aggregating many signatures into one.
Ed25519 (EdDSA)Solana, Cardano, many othersDeterministic signing, fast, designed to avoid implementation pitfalls.

The number of possible private keys is about 2256 (roughly 1077), so guessing someone's key is hopeless. The real-world failures are never the maths: they are weak random numbers, reused signature nonces, leaked seed phrases and phishing. You will implement ECDSA in chapter 12.

3.5 Addresses and wallets

An address is a shortened, checksummed hash of a public key. For Bitcoin's original format: RIPEMD160(SHA256(publicKey)), prefixed with a version byte and encoded in Base58Check, giving strings that start with 1. Newer formats start with bc1 (Bech32). Ethereum addresses are the last 20 bytes of the Keccak-256 hash of the public key, written as 0x….

A wallet does not contain coins. Coins exist only as entries on the chain. A wallet stores keys and builds and signs transactions. Modern wallets derive all keys from a single seed phrase of 12 or 24 words (BIP 39, hierarchical deterministic wallets per BIP 32). Whoever has the seed phrase controls every coin derived from it: back it up offline, never type it into a website, and never share it.

3.6 Proof of work: a puzzle built from hashing

Because hash outputs look random, the only way to find an input whose hash is below a chosen target is to try inputs until one works. A block header contains a free field, the nonce. Miners change the nonce and rehash, millions or trillions of times per second, until the header's hash falls below the network's target.

  • A smaller target means a harder puzzle. If the target allows d leading hexadecimal zeros, the expected number of attempts is 16d.
  • Verification takes one hash. The asymmetry (hard to find, trivial to check) is the whole point.
  • The winning hash is proof that real computation was spent, and the block commits to the exact transactions inside it, so changing them would require redoing the work.

4.2 What is inside a transaction?

A Bitcoin transaction has inputs (references to earlier outputs being spent, plus proof of the right to spend them: signatures and public keys, or a script satisfying the lock), and outputs (an amount in satoshis, where 1 BTC = 100,000,000 sats, plus a locking script that says who may spend it next). There is no "fee" field: the fee is simply sum(inputs) − sum(outputs), and miners collect the difference.

The first transaction of every block is the special coinbase transaction. It has no real inputs and creates the new coins (the block subsidy) plus collects fees. Its outputs cannot be spent until 100 blocks later.

An Ethereum transaction carries: nonce, to, value, data (calldata for a contract), gasLimit and fee fields (maxFeePerGas, maxPriorityFeePerGas), and the sender's signature (from which the sender's address is recovered).

A Bitcoin block is a header plus a list of transactions. The header is exactly 80 bytes and is the thing that gets hashed for proof of work:

4.5 Nodes: who does what

RoleWhat it does

Nodes connect to each other in a peer-to-peer mesh and forward whatever valid transactions and blocks they receive (gossip). A new node finds its first peers through hard-coded seeds or DNS seeds, then downloads and verifies the whole chain from the genesis block in the initial block download.

Miners do not make the rules

Miners (or validators) choose which valid transactions to include. They cannot make an invalid block valid: full nodes will reject it no matter how much work it contains. That is why the number of independently run full nodes matters for decentralisation, and why Bitcoin users fiercely defend the ability to run one on modest hardware.

5. Consensus mechanisms

⏱ 5 min Intermediate

A consensus mechanism answers one question over and over: which block is the next one? A good answer needs two guarantees, borrowed from distributed-systems theory:

  • Safety. Honest nodes never permanently disagree about a finalised block. (Nothing bad happens.)
  • Liveness. Valid transactions eventually get included. (Something good eventually happens.)

Open networks with no membership list add a third requirement: Sybil resistance, meaning influence must be tied to something scarce, not to the number of identities.

5.1 Proof of work and Nakamoto consensus

In proof of work (PoW), miners compete to find a nonce that makes the block header hash fall below the target. Whoever finds one first broadcasts the block and collects the reward. Nodes follow a simple fork-choice rule: adopt the valid chain with the most accumulated work (loosely, "the longest chain"). Because finding blocks is a random process with a mean of ten minutes, two miners occasionally find blocks at nearly the same time, creating a temporary fork.

Difficulty adjustment

Miners come and go, so the network automatically tunes the puzzle. Bitcoin recalculates the target every 2016 blocks (about two weeks): if the last 2016 blocks arrived faster than 10 minutes each, the target shrinks (harder); if slower, it grows. The change per adjustment is capped at a factor of four. Result: block time stays near ten minutes regardless of total hash power.

Why PoW is secure, and what it costs

  • Rewriting history means redoing all the work from the fork point and outpacing the rest of the network. The attacker pays real electricity for what honest miners get rewarded for.
  • The security budget is roughly what miners spend. As Bitcoin's subsidy halves over time, fees must eventually replace it, an open question debated in chapter 19.
  • Mining has industrialised: specialised chips (ASICs) and mining pools that share rewards. Pool operators construct blocks for many miners, which concentrates one kind of power even if hash power itself is widely owned.
  • Energy use is the price of the design, not a bug to be engineered away (more in chapter 19).

5.2 Proof of stake

Proof of stake (PoS) replaces burned electricity with locked capital. Participants deposit ("stake") coins as a bond. The protocol picks block proposers and committees pseudo-randomly with probability proportional to stake. Honest work earns rewards; provable misbehaviour is slashed, meaning part of the bond is destroyed. You will simulate this in chapter 14.

Ethereum's version (since the Merge in September 2022), nicknamed Gasper, combines two ideas:

  • LMD-GHOST: the fork-choice rule, which follows the branch with the most recent validator votes ("attestations").
  • Casper FFG: a finality gadget. Validators vote on checkpoints once per epoch (32 slots of 12 seconds, so 6.4 minutes). A checkpoint backed by two thirds of staked ether is justified; the next justified one finalises it. Reverting a finalised block would require at least one third of all stake to be slashed.

5.3 Other families

  • BFT-style PoS (Tendermint / Cosmos, HotStuff-derived chains). A known validator set votes in rounds. Blocks have instant finality (once committed, they are final) but progress halts if more than one third of voting power is offline or malicious. Favours consistency over availability.
  • Delegated PoS (EOS, Tron). Token holders vote for a small set of block producers (21 to 100). Fast, but small validator sets increase centralisation risk.
  • Proof of authority. Pre-approved identities sign blocks. Common in private and consortium networks.
  • Proof of History (Solana). A verifiable sequence of hashes acts as a cryptographic clock so validators agree on ordering without chatty messaging; it sits beneath a PoS voting protocol (Tower BFT).
  • Proof of space / time (Chia). Allocated disk space replaces computation as the scarce resource.
  • DAG-based protocols (Avalanche, Hedera). Instead of a single chain, nodes sample each other repeatedly or build a graph of events to converge on a result.

6. Bitcoin in depth

⏱ 5 min Intermediate

6.1 What Bitcoin set out to be

The 2008 whitepaper, Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System (nine pages, available at bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf), proposes electronic cash that can go directly from one party to another without a financial institution. Its pieces: digital signatures for ownership, a peer-to-peer network, a timestamp server realised as a chain of proof-of-work blocks, and a reward that pays participants to protect the system. The genesis block, mined on 3 January 2009, embeds a newspaper headline as a timestamp and as a statement of purpose: "The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks."

6.5 Fees, SegWit and Taproot

Block space is limited, so users bid with fees. A transaction's cost depends on its size in virtual bytes, not on the amount being sent. Upgrades have mostly been soft forks:

  • SegWit (August 2017). Moved signature ("witness") data into a separate structure with a discount. It fixed transaction malleability (third parties altering a transaction's ID), made Lightning practical and effectively raised capacity.
  • Taproot (November 2021). Adds Schnorr signatures and a way to commit to many spending conditions in one key; cooperative spends look identical on-chain, improving privacy and efficiency.
  • Replace-by-fee (RBF) and child-pays-for-parent (CPFP). Wallet techniques to bump a stuck transaction's fee.

Since 2023, "inscriptions" and token experiments (Ordinals, BRC-20) have used the witness space to store arbitrary data, sparking a lasting debate about what Bitcoin's block space is for.

6.6 The Lightning Network

Settling every coffee on a base layer that makes a block every ten minutes does not scale. The Lightning Network (Poon and Dryja, proposed 2015, live on the main network since 2018) moves frequent small payments off-chain:

  1. Two parties lock funds in a shared 2-of-2 multisig address (one on-chain transaction opens the channel).
  2. They exchange signed balance updates privately, any number of times, instantly and for almost no fee. Each update invalidates the last.
  3. Payments can be routed across a network of channels using hash-time-locked contracts (HTLCs) so intermediaries cannot steal funds.
  4. Closing the channel posts the final balances on-chain. If one party cheats by broadcasting an old state, the other can claim everything with a penalty transaction.

6.7 Governance and the block-size wars

Bitcoin has no CEO. Changes come as Bitcoin Improvement Proposals (BIPs), are implemented mostly in Bitcoin Core, and only take effect if enough miners signal readiness and, crucially, if economic users run the new software. The 2015 to 2017 dispute over raising the block size limit tested this: the faction that wanted bigger blocks split off as Bitcoin Cash on 1 August 2017, the SegWit2x compromise was abandoned in late 2017, and the main chain kept small blocks plus SegWit. It is the clearest demonstration that in Bitcoin, node operators and users, not miners, ultimately define the rules.

6.8 Satoshi Nakamoto

The inventor's identity is unknown. The pseudonym wrote the whitepaper and the first software, mined the early blocks (analyses of mining patterns suggest on the order of a million bitcoin, never moved), and stopped communicating in 2011, handing the project to others. Many people have been proposed or have claimed to be Satoshi; none has proven it by signing a message with the genesis-era keys.

7. Ethereum in depth

⏱ 5 min Intermediate

7.1 From "digital cash" to a world computer

Vitalik Buterin proposed Ethereum in late 2013 with a simple generalisation: instead of a ledger that only tracks coin ownership and has a limited script, build a blockchain with a general-purpose programming model. Developers deploy programs (smart contracts) to the chain, and every node executes them identically. Gavin Wood wrote the formal specification (the Yellow Paper) and Solidity's first design, and the network launched on 30 July 2015.

The result is best pictured as a single, global, shared computer with a public state: a database anyone can read, whose update rules are programs that everyone can inspect, and which only changes through signed, fee-paying transactions.

All accounts together form the world state, committed to by the stateRoot in every block header via a Merkle-Patricia trie. Executing a block means applying its transactions in order to the previous state to produce the next state: Ethereum is a state machine replicated across thousands of nodes.

7.3 The EVM and gas

The Ethereum Virtual Machine is a deterministic stack machine with 256-bit words, a few hundred opcodes, volatile memory and persistent storage. Contracts are normally written in Solidity (or Vyper) and compiled to EVM bytecode. Because anyone can submit a program, the network must stop infinite loops and price computation fairly. That is the job of gas.

  • Every opcode costs gas: adding two numbers is cheap, writing a new storage slot is expensive (roughly 20,000 gas), a simple ether transfer is 21,000 gas.
  • The sender sets a gas limit and a fee. If execution exceeds the limit it reverts (state changes are undone) but the gas already consumed is still paid.
  • Since the London upgrade (August 2021, EIP-1559): total fee = gas used × (base fee + priority tip). The base fee is set by the protocol, rises or falls up to 12.5% per block depending on how full the previous block was, and is burned (destroyed). The tip goes to the block proposer.

Because gas is the unit of both cost and limit, writing efficient contracts is not optimisation for its own sake: it decides whether your users can afford to use them.

7.4 Proof of stake in practice

  • Two layers. Since the Merge each node runs an execution client (runs the EVM; for example Geth, Nethermind, Besu, Reth) paired with a consensus client (runs the proof-of-stake protocol; for example Prysm, Lighthouse, Teku, Nimbus, Lodestar). Multiple independent implementations are a deliberate protection against a single client bug.
  • Time. Time is divided into 12-second slots; 32 slots form a 6.4-minute epoch. One validator proposes the block for each slot, and committees of validators attest to it.
  • Stake. Running a validator originally required exactly 32 ETH. The 2025 Pectra upgrade (EIP-7251) allowed one validator to carry up to 2,048 ETH, reducing overhead for large stakers.
  • Rewards and penalties. Validators earn issuance for attesting and proposing correctly, small penalties for being offline, and heavy slashing for provable double-signing. Staking withdrawals have been enabled since April 2023.
  • Issuance and burn. Issuance is far lower than under proof of work, and EIP-1559 burns base fees. When network use is high enough, more ether is burned than issued, making supply net-deflationary; in quiet periods it grows slowly.

MEV: the hidden market inside block ordering

Whoever orders a block's transactions can profit from the ordering: front-running a large trade, "sandwiching" it between two of the attacker's trades, capturing arbitrage, or liquidating loans. This extractable profit is maximal extractable value (MEV). In practice specialised builders compete to assemble the most profitable block and relays pass it to validators (MEV-Boost, since the Merge). MEV is a structural feature of any chain where transaction order is for sale, and managing it (private order flow, encrypted mempools, proposer-builder separation) is an active research area.

8. Smart contracts, tokens and DeFi

⏱ 5 min Intermediate 🐍 runnable Python

8.1 What a smart contract is (and isn't)

Nick Szabo coined the term in the 1990s with the picture of a vending machine: a mechanism that enforces an agreement (insert coins, get a snack) with no clerk. A smart contract is a program stored at an address on a blockchain; its code and state are public; it runs when a transaction calls one of its functions; and the result is verified by every node.

  • They are not smart (they do exactly what the code says, including its bugs) and not automatically legal contracts.
  • They are deterministic: no randomness from the clock, no web requests. To use outside data they need an oracle (a service such as Chainlink that posts data on-chain), which reintroduces a trust assumption.
  • By default they are immutable. Teams that want fixability use upgradeable proxy patterns, which hand the upgrade key's holder great power. Always ask who can upgrade.

8.2 A contract you can read

Here is a minimal escrow in Solidity. Notice the ordering inside release(): state is updated before money is sent. That order prevents the reentrancy bug you will exploit in chapter 15.

// SPDX-License-Identifier: MIT
pragma solidity ^0.8.24;

/// A buyer locks ether; only the buyer can release it to the seller.
contract Escrow {
    address public immutable buyer;
    address public immutable seller;
    uint256 public immutable amount;
    bool public released;

    constructor(address _seller) payable {
        require(msg.value > 0, "send funds");
        buyer = msg.sender;
        seller = _seller;
        amount = msg.value;
    }

    function release() external {
        require(msg.sender == buyer, "only buyer");
        require(!released, "already released");

        released = true;                                  // 1. effects first
        (bool ok, ) = seller.call{value: amount}("");     // 2. interaction last
        require(ok, "transfer failed");                   //    (revert undoes everything)
    }
}

        _move(msg.sender, to, value);
        return true;

        emit Approval(msg.sender, spender, value);
        return true;

        require(allowance[from][msg.sender] >= value, "allowance too low");
        allowance[from][msg.sender] -= value;
        _move(from, to, value);
        return true;
    }

    function _move(address from, address to, uint256 value) internal {
        require(balanceOf[from] >= value, "insufficient balance");
        balanceOf[from] -= value;     // Solidity 0.8 reverts on overflow/underflow
        balanceOf[to] += value;
        emit Transfer(from, to, value);
    }

8.4 Decentralised finance (DeFi)

DeFi rebuilds financial services as open, composable contracts. Because every contract is callable by every other contract, protocols snap together like "money legos".

Automated market makers (AMMs)

Traditional exchanges match buy and sell orders. An AMM such as Uniswap holds a pool of two tokens and prices trades with a formula. The classic constant-product rule keeps x × y = k, where x and y are the pool's reserves. Buying token Y with token X adds X to the pool and removes Y, moving the price along a curve. Larger trades relative to the pool move the price more: that is slippage. Liquidity providers deposit both tokens, earn a fee on every swap, and bear impermanent loss when the price ratio drifts from where they entered.

Constant-product AMM

Try selling 1, 10 and 100 X into a 1,000 X pool. Watch the slippage grow non-linearly, and see how the point slides along the hyperbola.

Lending and borrowing

Protocols such as Aave and Compound let users deposit assets to earn interest and borrow against them. Loans are over-collateralised (borrow 100 of value, post, say, 150), because there is no credit score and no court. If the collateral's value drops near the loan's value, anyone may liquidate the position and keep a bonus. Interest rates are set algorithmically from utilisation.

Other building blocks

  • Liquid staking. A token (such as stETH) that represents staked ether and stays tradable.
  • DAOs. Decentralised autonomous organisations manage a treasury and rules through token-holder votes executed by contracts. Practical problems: low turnout, whale control, legal ambiguity.

8.5 NFTs and tokenised assets

An NFT is a token with a unique ID. What you own is the token, which usually contains a link to metadata and an image stored elsewhere (often IPFS or a web server). The NFT proves a record of ownership on the chain; it does not by itself grant copyright, and if the linked file disappears the token survives but points at nothing. After CryptoKitties (late 2017) and a 2021 boom, trading volumes collapsed in 2022 to 2023, leaving a clearer picture: the durable uses are ticketing, memberships, game items and on-chain identity, while speculative profile-picture markets shrank sharply.

A growing area is real-world asset (RWA) tokenisation: representing money-market funds, treasuries, bonds or property as tokens. The chain handles transfer and settlement; the law and a legal issuer still define what the token entitles you to.

8.6 Limits to keep in mind

  • Code is law only until a bug appears; audits reduce, never eliminate, risk.
  • Public state means competitors and attackers see everything, including pending transactions.
  • Throughput and gas cost cap what is economical to compute on-chain, so heavy logic runs off-chain with proofs or oracles.

9. Scaling, interoperability and the chain zoo

⏱ 4 min Advanced

9.1 The trilemma

Vitalik Buterin popularised the blockchain trilemma: it is hard to maximise decentralisation, security and scalability at once. Making blocks bigger or faster raises the hardware needed to run a node, which shrinks the set of people who can verify, which erodes decentralisation. The pragmatic response is a layered design.

9.2 Layer 2 approaches

A rollup relies on a sequencer that orders transactions. Today most sequencers are centralised, a point of censorship risk, and many rollups still hold upgrade keys. Community trackers such as L2BEAT grade rollups by how much they have decentralised ("stages" 0 to 2).

Zero-knowledge proofs in one paragraph

A zero-knowledge proof convinces a verifier that a statement is true without revealing why. Systems called SNARKs and STARKs produce short proofs that are cheap to check even when the underlying computation was huge. For scaling this means "I executed 10,000 transactions correctly" can be verified in a single small check. For privacy it means proving you are over 18, or that you own a coin, without revealing who you are. Zcash pioneered private payments with SNARKs (2016); ZK proofs are now central to Ethereum's scaling.

9.3 Data availability and blobs

For anyone to verify or rebuild a rollup, its data must be published. Ethereum's 2024 EIP-4844 added blobs: large chunks of data that live on the consensus layer for only a few weeks, priced separately from ordinary gas, and designed to be cheap. Later upgrades extend this with data availability sampling so that each node checks only random pieces of every blob and still gets a high-confidence guarantee that the whole thing was published.

9.5 Bridges and interoperability

Moving assets between chains is hard, because neither chain can see the other. Typical bridges lock tokens on chain A and mint a wrapped version on chain B, trusting a set of validators, a multisig or a light client to attest to the lock. Bridges concentrate enormous value behind a small trust assumption, which is why they are repeatedly the largest hacks (chapter 17). Native cross-chain messaging with light-client verification (Cosmos IBC, and various ZK bridges) is more secure but harder to deploy.

9.6 Enterprise and permissioned ledgers

Not every blockchain is public. Hyperledger Fabric (Linux Foundation), R3 Corda and the Ethereum-derived Quorum/Besu target consortia of known firms: trade finance, supply-chain provenance, interbank settlement. They usually trade censorship resistance for speed, privacy and legal accountability. Many early enterprise pilots were quietly dropped because a shared database met the need; the survivors tend to be cases where several organisations genuinely need one shared source of truth and none can be the operator.

9.7 Not everything is a chain

Some distributed ledgers use other structures: IOTA's Tangle and Hedera's hashgraph are DAGs, and Algorand uses a pure PoS design with cryptographic sortition. They are all attempts to loosen the trilemma and are covered by the broader term DLT.

Check yourself

Q1. What is the key difference between an optimistic and a ZK rollup? Q2. Why are cross-chain bridges frequent hacking targets?

Answers. (1) Optimistic rollups assume validity and rely on fraud proofs during a challenge window; ZK rollups prove validity up front with a cryptographic proof. (2) They hold large pooled funds behind a small trust assumption (a few signers or a single contract), so one failure or bug compromises everything.

Example 2: a blockchain in 60 lines

The Block class hashes its own contents. mine() increments the nonce until the hash starts with enough zeros: that loop is proof of work. Blockchain.is_valid() performs the three checks every real node performs: the contents match the stored hash, the link to the parent is right, and the work requirement is met.

"""Example 2 - A blockchain from scratch: blocks, hash links, proof of work."""
import hashlib
import json
import time


class Block:
    def __init__(self, index, data, previous_hash, difficulty):
        self.index = index
        self.timestamp = time.time()
        self.data = data
        self.previous_hash = previous_hash
        self.difficulty = difficulty      # number of leading zero HEX digits
        self.nonce = 0
        self.hash = self.compute_hash()

    def compute_hash(self) -> str:
        header = json.dumps(
            [self.index, self.timestamp, self.data, self.previous_hash,
             self.difficulty, self.nonce],
            sort_keys=True,
        )
        return hashlib.sha256(header.encode()).hexdigest()

    def mine(self) -> int:
        """Search for a nonce whose hash starts with `difficulty` zeros."""
        prefix = "0" * self.difficulty
        attempts = 0
        while not self.hash.startswith(prefix):
            self.nonce += 1
            self.hash = self.compute_hash()
            attempts += 1
        return attempts


class Blockchain:
    def __init__(self, difficulty=4):
        self.difficulty = difficulty
        genesis = Block(0, "genesis", "0" * 64, difficulty)
        genesis.mine()
        self.chain = [genesis]

    def add_block(self, data) -> Block:
        block = Block(len(self.chain), data, self.chain[-1].hash, self.difficulty)
        block.mine()
        self.chain.append(block)
        return block

    def is_valid(self) -> bool:
        for prev, cur in zip(self.chain, self.chain[1:]):
            if cur.hash != cur.compute_hash():            # contents changed?
                return False
            if cur.previous_hash != prev.hash:            # link broken?
                return False
            if not cur.hash.startswith("0" * cur.difficulty):  # work missing?
                return False
        return True


if __name__ == "__main__":
    bc = Blockchain(difficulty=4)
    for payload in ["Alice pays Bob 5", "Bob pays Carol 2", "Carol pays Dan 1"]:
        started = time.time()
        block = bc.add_block(payload)

              f"hash={block.hash[:16]}...  {time.time() - started:.2f}s")

What to notice.

  • With difficulty 4 (four hex zeros) each block takes on average 16⁴ = 65,536 hashes, so the nonces and times vary wildly from run to run. That variance is why real block times are only on average ten minutes.
  • Changing block 1's data invalidates it. Recomputing its hash fixes block 1 but now block 2's previous_hash is stale. Only re-mining every later block restores validity. That is the cost of rewriting history, exactly as in the browser lab.
  • The honest network keeps extending the chain while the attacker redoes this work, which is why the attacker must also be faster than everyone else (chapter 17).

Example 3: Merkle trees and proofs

merkle_levels builds the tree bottom-up (duplicating the last hash when a level has an odd count, as Bitcoin does). merkle_proof collects the sibling at each level and verify recomputes the root from one leaf and its proof: precisely what a light client does.

"""Example 3 - Merkle tree: one root commits to many transactions."""
import hashlib


def h(data: bytes) -> bytes:
    return hashlib.sha256(hashlib.sha256(data).digest()).digest()   # sha256d


def merkle_levels(leaves: list[bytes]) -> list[list[bytes]]:
    """Return every level of the tree, from leaf hashes up to the root."""
    level = [h(x) for x in leaves]
    levels = [level]
    while len(level) > 1:
        if len(level) % 2 == 1:            # odd count: duplicate the last node
            level = level + [level[-1]]
        level = [h(level[i] + level[i + 1]) for i in range(0, len(level), 2)]
        levels.append(level)
    return levels


def merkle_proof(leaves: list[bytes], index: int) -> list[tuple[bytes, str]]:
    """Sibling hashes needed to rebuild the root for leaf `index`."""
    levels = merkle_levels(leaves)
    proof = []
    for level in levels[:-1]:
        if len(level) % 2 == 1:
            level = level + [level[-1]]
        sibling = index ^ 1                 # neighbour at this level
        side = "right" if sibling > index else "left"
        proof.append((level[sibling], side))
        index //= 2
    return proof


def verify(leaf: bytes, proof: list[tuple[bytes, str]], root: bytes) -> bool:
    node = h(leaf)
    for sibling, side in proof:
        node = h(node + sibling) if side == "right" else h(sibling + node)
    return node == root


if __name__ == "__main__":
    txs = [f"tx{i}: payment #{i}".encode() for i in range(1, 9)]   # 8 transactions

    root = levels[-1][0]
    print("levels (leaf -> root):", [len(l) for l in levels])
    print("merkle root          :", root.hex()[:32], "...")

    proof = merkle_proof(txs, 5)             # prove tx6 is in the block
    print("proof length         :", len(proof), "hashes (log2 of 8 = 3)")
    print("tx6 verifies         :", verify(txs[5], proof, root))
    print("forged tx fails      :", verify(b"tx6: payment #600", proof, root))

    txs[2] = b"tx3: payment #3 (edited)"     # change ONE transaction...
    print("root changes if edit :", merkle_levels(txs)[-1][0] != root)

A known Bitcoin quirk

Duplicating the last node means two different transaction lists can have the same Merkle root (CVE-2012-2459). Bitcoin nodes defend against it, and it is a good example of how a small design shortcut becomes a security issue.

12. Lab 2: keys, signatures and addresses

⏱ 4 min Advanced 🐍 runnable Python

Bitcoin and Ethereum both use the elliptic curve secp256k1: the set of points satisfying y² = x³ + 7 over a huge prime field. Points on a curve can be "added" with a geometric rule, and repeatedly adding a point G to itself k times gives k·G. Computing k·G from k is fast (about 256 doublings and additions); going back from k·G to k is believed infeasible. That asymmetry gives you a key pair:

  • private key d: a random integer from 1 to n−1 (about 2256)
  • public key Q = d·G, a point (compressed to 33 bytes)

The signature equations

To sign message hash z with private key d: pick a fresh random k, compute r = x-coordinate of k·G (mod n), then s = k⁻¹ (z + r·d) mod n. The signature is (r, s). To verify: compute w = s⁻¹, then the point u₁G + u₂Q where u₁ = z·w and u₂ = r·w; the signature is valid when that point's x-coordinate equals r. (Substituting Q = dG shows the point equals k·G, which is why it works.)

"""Example 4 - Keys, signatures and addresses, pure Python (secp256k1 / ECDSA).

Educational only: not constant-time, so never use it to protect real funds.
"""
import hashlib
import secrets

# --- secp256k1 curve parameters: y^2 = x^3 + 7 over the prime field P --------
P = 2**256 - 2**32 - 977
N = 0xFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFEBAAEDCE6AF48A03BBFD25E8CD0364141
G = (0x79BE667EF9DCBBAC55A06295CE870B07029BFCDB2DCE28D959F2815B16F81798,
     0x483ADA7726A3C4655DA4FBFC0E1108A8FD17B448A68554199C47D08FFB10D4B8)


def point_add(p1, p2):
    if p1 is None: return p2
    if p2 is None: return p1
    (x1, y1), (x2, y2) = p1, p2
    if x1 == x2 and (y1 + y2) % P == 0:
        return None                                   # point at infinity
    if p1 == p2:
        slope = 3 * x1 * x1 * pow(2 * y1, -1, P) % P  # tangent (doubling)
    else:
        slope = (y2 - y1) * pow(x2 - x1, -1, P) % P   # chord (addition)
    x3 = (slope * slope - x1 - x2) % P
    return (x3, (slope * (x1 - x3) - y1) % P)


def point_mul(k, point=G):
    """Double-and-add: the one-way function behind every public key."""
    result = None
    while k:
        if k & 1:
            result = point_add(result, point)
        point = point_add(point, point)
        k >>= 1
    return result


def new_private_key() -> int:
    return secrets.randbelow(N - 1) + 1


def public_key(priv: int) -> bytes:
    x, y = point_mul(priv)
    return bytes([2 + (y & 1)]) + x.to_bytes(32, "big")   # compressed, 33 bytes


def _decompress(pub: bytes):
    x = int.from_bytes(pub[1:], "big")
    y = pow((x**3 + 7) % P, (P + 1) // 4, P)               # modular square root
    return (x, y if (y & 1) == (pub[0] & 1) else P - y)


def sign(priv: int, message: bytes) -> tuple[int, int]:
    z = int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha256(message).digest(), "big")
    while True:
        k = secrets.randbelow(N - 1) + 1       # NEVER reuse or leak k
        r = point_mul(k)[0] % N
        s = pow(k, -1, N) * (z + r * priv) % N
        if r and s:
            return r, (N - s if s > N // 2 else s)          # low-s form


def verify(pub: bytes, message: bytes, sig: tuple[int, int]) -> bool:
    r, s = sig
    if not (1 <= r < N and 1 <= s < N):
        return False
    z = int.from_bytes(hashlib.sha256(message).digest(), "big")
    w = pow(s, -1, N)
    point = point_add(point_mul(z * w % N), point_mul(r * w % N, _decompress(pub)))
    return point is not None and point[0] % N == r

    while num:
        num, rem = divmod(num, 58)
        out = B58[rem] + out
    return "1" * (len(data) - len(data.lstrip(b"\0"))) + out

    ripemd = hashlib.new("ripemd160", hashlib.sha256(pub).digest()).digest()
    return base58check(b"\x00" + ripemd)

Why nonce reuse is fatal

If the same k is used for two signatures, an observer can solve two equations for the private key. This is not hypothetical. In 2010 the hackers' group fail0verflow showed Sony had signed PlayStation 3 firmware with a constant k, exposing the master key. In 2013 weak random number generation in Android's Java library led to stolen bitcoin from affected wallets. Modern libraries derive k deterministically (RFC 6979) so randomness failures cannot leak keys. Our teaching code uses secrets (a cryptographically secure source) but production code should use a vetted library such as libsecp256k1.

Never derive real keys from phrases

In the next chapter the demo derives keys from words like "demo-alice" so the output is reproducible. That is exactly what brain wallets do, and bots scan the chain constantly for any funds sent to addresses made from guessable phrases and sweep them within seconds. A key must come from a secure random source, with at least 128 bits of real entropy.

Deep dive: how the point arithmetic works

Adding two distinct points P₁ and P₂: draw the line through them; it hits the curve at a third point; reflect that point across the x-axis to get P₁ + P₂. Doubling a point uses the tangent line instead. In code (see point_add) the line's slope is (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1) for addition and 3x² / 2y for doubling, with division replaced by multiplication by a modular inverse. The "point at infinity" (None) is the identity element, like zero.

point_mul uses double-and-add: scan the bits of k; for each bit double the running point and, if the bit is 1, add P. A 256-bit scalar needs about 256 doublings and 128 additions, a few hundred operations, instead of 2256 steps.

This Python implementation is not constant-time: its running time depends on secret bits, which leaks information to an attacker who can measure timing. Real libraries avoid this.

"""Example 5 - A tiny cryptocurrency: signed transactions, mempool, mining, state.

Save next to ex04_keys.py (it provides the cryptography).
Account model (like Ethereum): each address has a balance and a nonce.

import hashlib
import json
from dataclasses import dataclass

from ex04_keys import address, new_private_key, public_key, sign, verify

BLOCK_REWARD = 50
DIFFICULTY_BITS = 14            # a valid block hash must be < 2**(256 - bits)


def sha256_hex(data: str) -> str:
    return hashlib.sha256(data.encode()).hexdigest()


# ----------------------------------------------------------------- transaction
@dataclass
class Tx:
    sender_pub: str      # hex public key of the sender
    to: str              # recipient address
    amount: int
    fee: int
    nonce: int           # sender's tx counter: stops replays
    sig: tuple = None

    @property
    def sender(self) -> str:
        return address(bytes.fromhex(self.sender_pub))

    def payload(self) -> bytes:
        return json.dumps([self.sender_pub, self.to, self.amount, self.fee,
                           self.nonce]).encode()

    @property
    def txid(self) -> str:
        return sha256_hex(self.payload().hex() + str(self.sig))

    def sign_with(self, priv: int):
        self.sig = sign(priv, self.payload())
        return self


# --------------------------------------------------------------------- ledger
class State:
    """Balances + nonces. Applying a block means replaying its transactions."""

    def __init__(self, balances=None, nonces=None):
        self.balances = dict(balances or {})
        self.nonces = dict(nonces or {})

    def copy(self):
        return State(self.balances, self.nonces)

    def check(self, tx: Tx) -> str | None:
        """Return a reason string if the transaction is invalid, else None."""
        if tx.amount <= 0 or tx.fee < 0:
            return "bad amount"
        if not verify(bytes.fromhex(tx.sender_pub), tx.payload(), tx.sig):
            return "bad signature"
        if tx.nonce != self.nonces.get(tx.sender, 0) + 1:
            return "bad nonce (replay or gap)"
        if self.balances.get(tx.sender, 0) < tx.amount + tx.fee:
            return "insufficient funds"
        return None

    def apply(self, tx: Tx):
        self.balances[tx.sender] -= tx.amount + tx.fee
        self.balances[tx.to] = self.balances.get(tx.to, 0) + tx.amount
        self.nonces[tx.sender] = tx.nonce


def merkle_root(txids: list[str]) -> str:
    level = txids or [sha256_hex("")]
    while len(level) > 1:
        if len(level) % 2:
            level = level + [level[-1]]
        level = [sha256_hex(level[i] + level[i + 1]) for i in range(0, len(level), 2)]
    return level[0]


@dataclass
class Block:
    height: int
    prev_hash: str
    miner: str
    txs: list
    nonce: int = 0

    def header_hash(self) -> str:
        root = merkle_root([t.txid for t in self.txs])
        return sha256_hex(f"{self.height}|{self.prev_hash}|{self.miner}|{root}|{self.nonce}")

    def meets_target(self) -> bool:
        return int(self.header_hash(), 16) < 2 ** (256 - DIFFICULTY_BITS)


class Chain:
    def __init__(self, genesis_balances):
        self.blocks = [Block(0, "0" * 64, "genesis", [])]
        self.state = State(genesis_balances)
        self.mempool: list[Tx] = []

    # -- accepting transactions into the mempool
    def submit(self, tx: Tx) -> str:
        trial = self.state.copy()                       # simulate pending ones first
        for pending in self.mempool:
            trial.apply(pending)
        problem = trial.check(tx)
        if problem:
            return f"REJECTED ({problem})"
        self.mempool.append(tx)
        return "accepted into mempool"

    # -- mining: pick transactions, add reward + fees, search for a nonce
    def mine(self, miner_address: str) -> Block:
        block = Block(len(self.blocks), self.blocks[-1].header_hash(),
                      miner_address, list(self.mempool))
        while not block.meets_target():
            block.nonce += 1
        self.add_block(block)
        return block

    # -- full validation of any incoming block (what every node does)
    def add_block(self, block: Block):
        assert block.prev_hash == self.blocks[-1].header_hash(), "wrong parent"
        assert block.meets_target(), "insufficient proof of work"
        trial = self.state.copy()
        fees = 0
        for tx in block.txs:
            problem = trial.check(tx)
            assert problem is None, f"invalid tx in block: {problem}"
            trial.apply(tx)
            fees += tx.fee
        trial.balances[block.miner] = trial.balances.get(block.miner, 0) + BLOCK_REWARD + fees
        self.state = trial
        self.blocks.append(block)
        self.mempool = [t for t in self.mempool if t not in block.txs]

    def balance(self, addr: str) -> int:
        return self.state.balances.get(addr, 0)

The state transition function

Notice add_block. It does not trust the miner: it copies the state, replays every transaction through check and apply, and only then replaces the real state. This "validate everything, apply atomically" pattern is how every node in every blockchain treats every block. Ethereum's whole execution layer is this function with a bigger rulebook (the EVM).

The UTXO model

Bitcoin does not keep account balances; it keeps a set of unspent outputs. A balance is just the sum of the outputs you can unlock. This example drops signatures to isolate the accounting: spend whole outputs, create new ones, send the remainder back to yourself as change, and anything left over is the fee.

"""Example 6 - The UTXO model (Bitcoin): coins are spent whole, change comes back.

Signatures are omitted here to keep the focus on the accounting.
"""
import hashlib
from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Output:
    owner: str
    value: int


@dataclass
class Tx:
    inputs: list          # list of (txid, output_index) being spent
    outputs: list         # list of Output

    @property
    def txid(self) -> str:
        blob = repr((self.inputs, self.outputs)).encode()
        return hashlib.sha256(blob).hexdigest()[:10]


@dataclass
class UTXOSet:
    unspent: dict = field(default_factory=dict)   # (txid, index) -> Output

    def add_coinbase(self, owner, value) -> Tx:
        tx = Tx([], [Output(owner, value)])
        for i, out in enumerate(tx.outputs):
            self.unspent[(tx.txid, i)] = out
        return tx

    def apply(self, tx: Tx, spender: str) -> int:
        """Validate and apply. Returns the fee (inputs - outputs)."""
        seen, total_in = set(), 0
        for ref in tx.inputs:
            if ref in seen:
                raise ValueError("input listed twice")
            if ref not in self.unspent:
                raise ValueError(f"{ref} unknown or ALREADY SPENT (double spend)")
            if self.unspent[ref].owner != spender:
                raise ValueError("not your coin")
            seen.add(ref)
            total_in += self.unspent[ref].value
        total_out = sum(o.value for o in tx.outputs)
        if total_out > total_in:
            raise ValueError("outputs exceed inputs")
        for ref in tx.inputs:
            del self.unspent[ref]
        for i, out in enumerate(tx.outputs):
            self.unspent[(tx.txid, i)] = out
        return total_in - total_out

    def balance(self, owner) -> int:
        return sum(o.value for o in self.unspent.values() if o.owner == owner)

Example 7: nodes, a fork and a reorganisation

This simulation has two nodes that gossip chains to each other. The rule that makes the whole network converge is in Node.receive: adopt a candidate chain only if it is valid and has more total work than yours. The script then partitions the network, lets both sides mine conflicting blocks, heals the partition and watches the lighter branch lose.

"""Example 7 - Nodes, forks and the heaviest-chain rule (Nakamoto consensus)."""
import hashlib

BITS = 12                                   # difficulty: hash < 2**(256-BITS)
TARGET = 2 ** (256 - BITS)
WORK_PER_BLOCK = 2 ** BITS                  # expected hashes to find one block


def block_hash(prev: str, data: str, nonce: int) -> str:
    return hashlib.sha256(f"{prev}|{data}|{nonce}".encode()).hexdigest()


def mine(prev: str, data: str) -> dict:
    nonce = 0
    while int(block_hash(prev, data, nonce), 16) >= TARGET:
        nonce += 1
    return {"prev": prev, "data": data, "nonce": nonce,
            "hash": block_hash(prev, data, nonce)}


GENESIS = {"prev": "0" * 64, "data": "genesis", "nonce": 0,
           "hash": block_hash("0" * 64, "genesis", 0)}


def valid_chain(chain: list[dict]) -> bool:
    if chain[0] != GENESIS:
        return False
    for parent, blk in zip(chain, chain[1:]):
        if blk["prev"] != parent["hash"]:
            return False
        if blk["hash"] != block_hash(blk["prev"], blk["data"], blk["nonce"]):
            return False
        if int(blk["hash"], 16) >= TARGET:
            return False
    return True


def total_work(chain: list[dict]) -> int:
    return (len(chain) - 1) * WORK_PER_BLOCK


class Node:
    def __init__(self, name):
        self.name = name
        self.chain = [GENESIS]
        self.peers: list["Node"] = []

    def mine_block(self, data):
        blk = mine(self.chain[-1]["hash"], data)
        self.chain.append(blk)
        print(f"  {self.name} mined '{data}' on top of {blk['prev'][:8]}")
        self.broadcast()

    def broadcast(self):
        for peer in self.peers:
            peer.receive(self.chain, sender=self.name)

    def receive(self, candidate: list[dict], sender: str):
        """Adopt the candidate only if it is valid AND has more total work."""
        if valid_chain(candidate) and total_work(candidate) > total_work(self.chain):
            dropped = [b["data"] for b in self.chain if b not in candidate]
            self.chain = list(candidate)
            tag = f" (reorg! orphaned: {dropped})" if dropped else ""
            print(f"  {self.name} switched to {sender}'s chain, "
                  f"height {len(self.chain) - 1}{tag}")

    def tip(self):
        return [b["data"] for b in self.chain[1:]]

    print("   B sees:", b.tip())
    print("   agree? ", a.chain == b.chain)
    print("   'Bob->Carol 2' was orphaned: it never became final history,")
    print("   which is why exchanges wait for several confirmations.")

  • While partitioned, nodes A and B each believe in a different block 2. Both are legitimate to their own side. There is no "truth" yet.
  • When B finds a third block, its branch has more work. After reconnection, A reorganises: it drops its own block 2, which is orphaned, and adopts B's chain. The payment inside the orphaned block returns to limbo and must be re-included.

Example 8: proof of stake and slashing

In proof of stake, "mining" becomes a weighted lottery over stake and the penalty for cheating is losing money. Three small experiments:

  1. Choose the proposer for 10,000 slots with probability proportional to stake and check the frequencies match.
  2. Show that signing two different blocks in one slot is provable and gets penalised.
  3. Compute the capital an attacker must put at risk to threaten finality.
"""Example 8 - Proof of stake in miniature: stake-weighted proposers and slashing."""
import random
from collections import Counter

random.seed(2026)                        # reproducible demo

stakes = {"alice": 32, "bob": 64, "carol": 96, "dave": 160}   # ETH-like units
total = sum(stakes.values())
print("total stake:", total)

# --- 1. Choose a block proposer each slot, weighted by stake -----------------
# Real Ethereum uses RANDAO + a shuffling algorithm, but the principle holds:
# the chance of being picked is proportional to the stake at risk.
slots = 10_000
picks = Counter(random.choices(list(stakes), weights=list(stakes.values()), k=slots))
print(f"\nproposer frequency over {slots:,} slots")
for name, stake in stakes.items():
    print(f"  {name:<6} stake share {stake / total:6.1%}   picked {picks[name] / slots:6.1%}")

# --- 2. Slashing: misbehaviour burns the stake --------------------------------
class Validator:
    def __init__(self, name, stake):
        self.name, self.stake, self.slashed = name, stake, False
        self.signed = {}                      # slot -> block hash already signed

    def sign(self, slot, block_hash):
        if slot in self.signed and self.signed[slot] != block_hash:
            self.slash("double-signing at slot %d" % slot)
        self.signed[slot] = block_hash

    def slash(self, reason, fraction=0.5):
        penalty = self.stake * fraction
        self.stake -= penalty
        self.slashed = True
        print(f"  SLASHED {self.name}: -{penalty:g} for {reason}")


print("\nslashing demo")
v = Validator("mallory", 32)
v.sign(slot=100, block_hash="0xaaa")      # honest first vote

"""Example 9 - Smart-contract ideas in Python: a token, an AMM, a reentrancy bug.

Real contracts run on the EVM in Solidity/Vyper; here we simulate the logic.
"""


class Revert(Exception):
    """Raised by require(): undo everything the call did."""


def require(condition, message):
    if not condition:
        raise Revert(message)


# ----------------------------------------------------------------- 1. a token
class Token:
    """A minimal ERC-20-style fungible token."""

    def __init__(self, name, supply, owner):
        self.name = name
        self.balances = {owner: supply}
        self.allowance = {}
        self.events = []

    def transfer(self, sender, to, amount):
        require(self.balances.get(sender, 0) >= amount, "insufficient balance")
        self.balances[sender] -= amount
        self.balances[to] = self.balances.get(to, 0) + amount
        self.events.append(("Transfer", sender, to, amount))

    def approve(self, owner, spender, amount):
        self.allowance[(owner, spender)] = amount

    def transfer_from(self, spender, owner, to, amount):
        require(self.allowance.get((owner, spender), 0) >= amount, "allowance too low")
        self.allowance[(owner, spender)] -= amount
        self.transfer(owner, to, amount)


# ------------------------------------------------- 2. constant-product AMM pool
class AMM:
    """Uniswap-v2-style pool: reserves X * Y = k stays (almost) constant."""

    def __init__(self, x, y, fee_bps=30):
        self.x, self.y, self.fee_bps = x, y, fee_bps

    def price(self):
        return self.y / self.x                    # spot price of X in Y

    def swap_x_for_y(self, dx):
        dx_after_fee = dx * (10_000 - self.fee_bps)      # integer maths, like Solidity
        dy = dx_after_fee * self.y // (self.x * 10_000 + dx_after_fee)
        self.x += dx
        self.y -= dy
        return dy


# ------------------------------------------------ 3. the reentrancy bug (DAO)
class VulnerableBank:
    def __init__(self):
        self.balances, self.vault = {}, 0

    def deposit(self, who, amount):
        self.balances[who] = self.balances.get(who, 0) + amount
        self.vault += amount

    def withdraw(self, who):
        amount = self.balances.get(who, 0)
        require(amount > 0, "nothing to withdraw")
        self.vault -= amount
        who.receive(amount)                  # (1) INTERACTION first: hands control away
        self.balances[who] = 0               # (2) EFFECT last: far too late


class SafeBank(VulnerableBank):
    def withdraw(self, who):
        amount = self.balances.get(who, 0)
        require(amount > 0, "nothing to withdraw")
        self.balances[who] = 0               # EFFECT first ...
        self.vault -= amount
        who.receive(amount)                  # ... INTERACTION last (checks-effects-interactions)


class Attacker:
    def __init__(self, bank):
        self.bank, self.stolen, self.depth = bank, 0, 0

    def __repr__(self):
        return "attacker"

    def receive(self, amount):               # like a Solidity receive() fallback
        self.stolen += amount
        if self.depth < 20 and self.bank.vault >= amount:
            self.depth += 1
            try:
                self.bank.withdraw(self)     # re-enter before balance was zeroed!
            except Revert:
                pass                         # the safe bank refuses the second call

The exploit step by step

Four honest users have deposited 25 coins each (100 in the vault). The attacker deposits 10 and calls withdraw.

  1. VulnerableBank.withdraw reads the attacker's balance (10) and sends 10 coins by calling the attacker's receive function. It has not yet set the balance to 0.
  2. The attacker's receive immediately calls withdraw again. The bank still sees a balance of 10 and pays another 10, and so on. The loop continues until the vault is empty.
  3. The attacker walks away with 110 while having deposited 10. The honest users still hold "balances" of 100 on paper but the vault contains 0.
  4. SafeBank only reorders two lines: it sets the balance to 0 before sending. When the attacker tries to re-enter, the balance is already zero and the call reverts.
Checks-effects-interactions

Do all checks first, then update your own effects (state), and only then interact with external contracts or addresses. Interacting hands control to code you do not own; assume it will call you back. Additional defences: reentrancy guard modifiers (nonReentrant), pull-over-push payments, and limiting what any single contract holds.

The real DAO exploit worked on the same principle, using splitDAO to withdraw repeatedly before the balance was reduced, and roughly 3.6 million ETH moved. That event is why the Solidity community treats this ordering as a fundamental rule.

def sha256d(data: bytes) -> bytes:
    return hashlib.sha256(hashlib.sha256(data).digest()).digest()


def bits_to_target(bits: int) -> int:
    """Compact 'bits' -> 256-bit target. Hash must be numerically below it."""
    exponent, mantissa = bits >> 24, bits & 0xFFFFFF
    return mantissa * 256 ** (exponent - 3)


def inspect_header(header_hex: str) -> None:
    raw = bytes.fromhex(header_hex)
    assert len(raw) == 80, "a Bitcoin block header is exactly 80 bytes"
    version, prev, merkle, timestamp, bits, nonce = struct.unpack("<I32s32sIII", raw)
    block_hash = sha256d(raw)[::-1].hex()          # displayed byte-reversed
    target = bits_to_target(bits)
    difficulty = bits_to_target(0x1D00FFFF) / target

    print("  version      :", version)
    print("  prev block   :", prev[::-1].hex()[:24] + "...")
    print("  merkle root  :", merkle[::-1].hex()[:24] + "...")
    print("  timestamp    :", timestamp)
    print("  bits         :", hex(bits))
    print("  nonce        :", nonce)
    print("  block hash   :", block_hash)
    print("  leading zeros:", len(block_hash) - len(block_hash.lstrip("0")), "hex digits")
    print("  hash < target:", int(block_hash, 16) < target, "<- this IS proof of work")
    print(f"  difficulty   : {difficulty:,.0f}")


def fetch(path: str) -> str:
    with urllib.request.urlopen("https://blockstream.info/api" + path, timeout=15) as r:
        return r.read().decode()

17.1 Attacks on consensus

The 51% attack (majority attack)

If one party controls more than half of a proof-of-work chain's hash power (or, in proof of stake, enough stake to control the fork choice or finality), they can out-build the honest chain.

An attacker with a majority canAn attacker with a majority cannot
Reverse their own recent transactions (double-spend after receiving goods)Spend coins they don't own (signatures still need private keys)
Censor chosen transactions or other miners' blocksChange the consensus rules; full nodes reject invalid blocks

The attack has repeatedly hit smaller proof-of-work coins whose hash power can be rented by the hour: Ethereum Classic (2019 and 2020), Bitcoin Gold (2018 and 2020) and Verge (2018) all suffered deep reorganisations and stolen exchange deposits. Against Bitcoin the cost is enormous, and the attacker would likely destroy the value of the very asset they hold or the hardware they bought.

How many confirmations are enough?

The whitepaper models a double-spend as a race between an attacker with fraction q of the hash power and the honest network. After z confirmations the attacker must catch up from z blocks behind. The probability falls exponentially for any attacker below 50%, but they are certain to succeed eventually at 50% or more. Here is the formula from the paper (section 11) in ex11_attack.py:

"""Example 11 - How likely is a double-spend? (formula from the Bitcoin whitepaper, section 11)

An attacker controlling fraction q of the hash power secretly mines a competing chain.
After the merchant waits for z confirmations, what is the chance the attacker catches up?
"""
from math import exp, factorial


def catch_up_probability(q: float, z: int) -> float:
    p = 1.0 - q
    if q >= p:
        return 1.0                              # a majority attacker always wins eventually
    lam = z * q / p                             # expected attacker progress while honest nodes mine z blocks
    total = 0.0
    for k in range(z + 1):
        poisson = exp(-lam) * lam**k / factorial(k)
        total += poisson * (1 - (q / p) ** (z - k))
    return 1.0 - total

17.5 The quantum question

A large, fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could derive private keys from public keys on the elliptic-curve schemes used today. Grover's algorithm only halves the effective strength of hash functions (SHA-256 stays comfortably strong). No such machine exists at the scale needed, but because blockchain data is permanent, it is already a planning topic. Mitigations: migrating to post-quantum signature schemes (NIST standardised its first in 2024), not reusing addresses (an unspent address that has never revealed its public key is better protected), and giving the community time to coordinate a migration.

A defender's checklist
  1. Decide what you are protecting and from whom (theft, loss, coercion, a bad contract).
  2. Hold significant funds with keys you control, on a hardware device; keep the seed offline in two physical places.
  3. Use separate wallets for savings and for experiments.
  4. Read what you sign. Prefer wallets that simulate transactions and show readable data.
  5. Avoid unlimited approvals; revoke the ones you no longer need.
  6. Assume any unsolicited message offering money, support or "recovery" is a scam.
  7. Test with a small amount first; verify the full address, not just its ends.
Check yourself

Q1. Can a 51% attacker steal bitcoin from a wallet they do not control? Q2. Using the table, how many confirmations make an attacker with 25% of the hash power succeed with probability below 1%?

Answers. (1) No: spending still requires a valid signature from the owner's key. They can reverse their own payments and censor. (2) 10 confirmations (0.84%).

⏱ 3 min Beginner

18.1 Pseudonymous, not anonymous

Bitcoin and Ethereum addresses are not tied to names, but every transaction is public forever. Analysis firms link addresses together with heuristics: inputs spent together probably belong to one owner; the change output usually returns to the sender; deposits to a known exchange reveal identity because the exchange knows its customers. A single slip (posting your address, buying at a regulated exchange, an ENS name) can connect your entire history to you.

18.3 Seed phrase hygiene

  • Write the 12 or 24 words on paper or metal, in order, offline. Never photograph, email, cloud-sync or type them into any website.
  • Keep at least two copies in separate secure places; plan how an heir could find them without being able to steal them early.
  • No legitimate support person, airdrop or app will ever ask for your seed phrase.
  • A passphrase ("25th word") adds a second secret, protects against a stolen backup, and creates a new risk if you forget it.
  • Test recovery with a small amount: restore the wallet on a different device before relying on the backup.

18.4 Scam patterns and red flags

Guaranteed returns

Nobody can promise fixed profit. Ponzi-style "yield" and "staking" platforms rely on new deposits.

Rug pulls

Developers raise money for a token, then drain the liquidity or mint themselves a fortune.

Pig-butchering

A stranger builds a long friendship or romance online, then steers you to a fake trading platform.

Giveaways and impersonation

"Send 1, get 2 back"; fake celebrity or support accounts.

Recovery scams

Services that claim they can retrieve stolen funds for an upfront fee.

Urgency and secrecy

"Act now", "tell no one", "connect your wallet to claim". All strong signals to stop.

Irreversibility cuts both ways

On public chains there is no chargeback and no customer-service desk that can reverse a signed transaction. That is a feature for censorship resistance and a hazard for a beginner. Practise on a test network first, then start small.

Check yourself

Q1. You received an email from "wallet support" asking you to "verify" your recovery phrase. What do you do? Q2. Why can a company holding your keys still be called a risk even if it has strong security?

Answers. (1) Ignore it, never share the phrase, and report the message; real support never needs it. (2) Custodians face operational mistakes, legal seizure, insolvency and misuse of funds in addition to hacking, and you only have an IOU.

19. Economics, energy, regulation and real use cases

⏱ 5 min Intermediate

19.1 Money, scarcity and the security budget

Bitcoin's design makes scarcity enforceable by software: nobody, not even the largest miner, can issue beyond the schedule without every node rejecting the block. Whether that scarcity gives the coin lasting value is an empirical and philosophical argument that this guide does not settle. The two common framings:

  • Store of value ("digital gold"). Value comes from credible scarcity, durability and no counterparty. Evidence for: a long track record of operating without failure. Evidence against: very high volatility compared with the assets it is compared to.
  • Medium of exchange. Value comes from being used to pay. Base-layer fees and volatility make small everyday purchases awkward; stablecoins have taken over much of the payments role.

The monetary base is only half the economics. A proof-of-work chain pays for its security through the subsidy plus fees. Each halving shrinks the subsidy, so the long-run security of Bitcoin depends on fee revenue growing enough to keep attackers' cost high. Whether it will is an open question that careful people disagree about.

19.2 Token economics (tokenomics) and how to read them

Every token has a design: how many exist, who got them, how new ones are created, what the token is needed for. When you evaluate a token, ask:

What gives the token demand beyond speculation (fees, collateral, governance with real power, staking yield from real revenue)?A token that is only needed to hold the token is circular
Where does yield come from?If you can't find a source other than new buyers, it is a transfer, not a return
Who can change the rules or upgrade the contracts?Admin keys are a trust assumption

19.3 Energy and the environment

Proof of work converts electricity into security, deliberately. Estimates of Bitcoin's electricity use vary with assumptions about hardware efficiency and the hash rate; the Cambridge Bitcoin Electricity Consumption Index (ccaf.io) publishes a range, and the figure is comparable to that of a mid-sized country. Perspectives:

  • Critique. It is a lot of energy for a payment system that processes few transactions per second, and mining hardware becomes e-waste as it is superseded.
  • Defence. The cost is what makes rewriting history expensive, so it should be compared with the cost of securing the traditional financial system and gold, not with a payment network's throughput. Miners seek the cheapest power, which can include otherwise-wasted or stranded energy and flexible loads that help grid balancing, though the net climate effect depends on the energy mix and remains debated.
  • Proof of stake. Ethereum's 2022 Merge cut the chain's energy use by more than 99.9%, showing there is a design alternative if one accepts its different security assumptions.

19.4 How decentralised is it, really?

Decentralisation is a measurable claim. Useful indicators:

  • Nakamoto coefficient. The smallest number of independent entities that could together control a majority (or the attack threshold) of hash power, stake or validators. Higher is better.
  • Node diversity. How many full nodes exist, in how many jurisdictions and hosting providers (cloud concentration is common); how many independent client implementations.
  • Governance concentration. Who writes the software, and who can approve or veto changes? Who holds admin keys on applications?
  • Economic concentration. How much supply sits in a few large wallets and exchanges.

19.5 Criticisms, taken seriously

  • Volatility and speculation. Large swings make crypto assets hard to use as units of account and have wiped out many retail buyers.
  • Fraud and misuse. Scams, ransomware payments, sanctions evasion and theft are real. Blockchain analytics firms estimate illicit activity is a small percentage of volume, but absolute amounts are large, and public ledgers also make tracing easier than with cash.
  • Complexity and usability. Irreversible mistakes, jargon and key management are hostile to ordinary users.
  • Inequality. Early holders and large stakers control a disproportionate share of supply and, therefore, influence.
  • Much blockchain hype did not survive contact with reality. Many "blockchain for X" pilots of 2016 to 2019 were shelved because a conventional database did the job.

CBDCs, stablecoins and crypto are different things

A central bank digital currency is a digital form of national money issued and controlled by the central bank, typically on a permissioned system. A stablecoin is a private token pegged to a currency. A cryptocurrency like Bitcoin has no issuer. They share technology (ledgers, tokens) but differ in who is in charge, and therefore in privacy, censorship resistance and policy impact.

Fifteen misconceptions, corrected

  1. "Blockchain is Bitcoin." Bitcoin is one blockchain among thousands.
  2. "Blockchain data is private/encrypted." Public chains are transparent by design.
  3. "Bitcoin is anonymous." It is pseudonymous and highly traceable.
  4. "Miners solve complex maths." They guess nonces in a hash lottery; there is no maths insight to gain.
  5. "The blockchain stores the coins." It stores records; wallets hold keys.
  6. "Immutable means nothing can ever change." It means rewriting is very costly; rules can change by social consensus.
  7. "A blockchain solves trust." It relocates trust to code, cryptography and incentives.
  8. "More decentralised is always better." It trades off speed, cost and sometimes usability; match to the problem.
  9. "Smart contracts are legal contracts." They are programs; legal force is a separate matter.
  10. "Proof of stake is just the rich ruling." It has concentration risk, but it also has slashing and open entry; proof of work has pools and ASIC manufacturers with their own concentration.
  11. "Hash power or stake owners can change the rules." Full nodes and users decide what counts as valid.
  12. "NFTs are the images." They are tokens that usually link to images.
  13. "Layer 2 = a different, riskier thing." Rollups inherit L1 security to a degree that depends on their design; read their assumptions.
  14. "If I lose my password, support can recover it." For self-custody, nobody can recover a lost key.
  15. "It's all a scam." A lot of it is, and a lot of it isn't. Judge each project by its mechanism, not by the sector.

Reading a block explorer

Open a Bitcoin explorer (for example mempool.space or blockstream.info) or an Ethereum one (etherscan.io), search for a recent transaction hash and check:

  • Status and confirmations: pending, success, failed (Ethereum failed transactions still burn gas).
  • From / To, the value, and the fee (in sats/vB or gwei).
  • Inputs and outputs (Bitcoin): which output is change?
  • Input data and logs (Ethereum): which function was called, which events (such as ERC-20 Transfer) were emitted?
  • Block height and timestamp, and how deep it is now.

A website that lets you search blocks, transactions and addresses on a chain.

Newly created coins paid to the block producer, plus transaction fees.

Large temporary data attached to Ethereum blocks (EIP-4844), used by rollups for cheap data availability.

A system moving assets or messages between two blockchains, usually by locking and minting wrapped tokens.

CBDC

The first transaction in a Bitcoin block; it creates the subsidy and collects fees. (Not the exchange of the same name.)

Property that it is infeasible to find two different inputs with the same hash.

One additional block built on top of the block containing a transaction.
Consensus
The process by which nodes agree on the single valid history.
Cold wallet
A wallet whose keys are kept offline.
DAO
Decentralised autonomous organisation: members govern shared funds or rules by token-weighted votes executed by contracts.
DeFi
Decentralised finance: financial services (trading, lending, derivatives) built from smart contracts.
Difficulty
A measure of how hard it is to find a valid block, inversely proportional to the target.
Digital signature
Data that proves a message was authorised by the holder of a private key, verifiable with the public key.
Double spend
Spending the same digital coin twice. Preventing it is the core job of a blockchain.

Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm, used by Bitcoin and Ethereum accounts on the curve secp256k1.
EIP / BIP
Ethereum / Bitcoin Improvement Proposals: numbered design documents for protocol changes.
EOA
Externally owned account: an Ethereum account controlled by a private key.
ERC-20 / ERC-721
Ethereum standards for fungible tokens and non-fungible tokens.
EVM
Ethereum Virtual Machine: the deterministic runtime that executes smart-contract bytecode.
Fork
A divergence in the chain: temporary (two blocks at once) or permanent (a rule change that splits the network).
Finality
The point at which a block can no longer be reverted, probabilistically or by economic guarantee.
Flash loan
An uncollateralised loan borrowed and repaid within a single transaction.
Full node
A node that downloads and independently validates every block and transaction.
Gas
The unit measuring computation on Ethereum; the sender pays fees in gas times price.
Genesis block
The very first block of a chain, hard-coded into the software.
Halving
The scheduled 50% cut of Bitcoin's block subsidy every 210,000 blocks.
Hash function
A one-way function mapping any input to a fixed-size fingerprint (SHA-256, Keccak-256).
Hash rate
The number of hash attempts per second performed by a miner or the whole network.
HD wallet
Hierarchical deterministic wallet: all keys derive from one seed (BIP 32).
Layer 1 / Layer 2
The base blockchain / a protocol built on top that inherits its security (rollups, channels).
Lightning Network
Bitcoin's layer-2 network of payment channels.

A client that verifies payments using block headers and Merkle proofs, not full blocks.

Forced sale of a borrower's collateral when its value falls below the required ratio.
Mainnet / testnet
The real network with valuable coins / a parallel network with worthless coins for experimenting.
Mempool
A node's waiting room of valid, unconfirmed transactions.
Merkle tree / root
A binary tree of hashes whose single root commits to a large set of data.
MEV
Maximal extractable value: profit available from ordering, inserting or censoring transactions in a block.
Miner
A participant who performs proof of work to produce blocks.
Multisig
An address requiring signatures from M of N keys to spend.
Nakamoto coefficient
The minimum number of independent entities needed to compromise a network's majority.
NFT
Non-fungible token: a unique token identifying an item, usually pointing to external metadata.
Node
A computer running blockchain software and taking part in the peer-to-peer network.

In mining, the number varied to search for a valid hash. In Ethereum accounts, a per-account transaction counter.

A rollup assuming batches are valid unless a fraud proof shows otherwise.
Oracle
A service that brings off-chain data (prices, events) onto a blockchain.
Orphan / stale block
A valid block that ended up outside the main chain.
Private key
The secret number that authorises spending. Never share it.

Consensus where block producers are chosen and secured by locked capital.
Proof of work (PoW)
Consensus where block producers must show expended computation by solving a hash puzzle.
Public key
The key derived from the private key that others use to verify signatures.
Reentrancy
A vulnerability where an external call lets the callee re-enter the caller before its state is updated.
Reorg
Reorganisation: nodes switch to a heavier competing chain, discarding blocks.
Rollup
A layer 2 that executes transactions off the base layer and posts compressed data and proofs to it.

The smallest bitcoin unit, 0.00000001 BTC.

12 or 24 words encoding the master secret from which a wallet's keys derive (BIP 39).

Segregated Witness: a 2017 Bitcoin upgrade moving signature data into a separate structure.
Sequencer
The operator that orders and batches transactions on a rollup.
Slashing
Destroying part of a validator's stake as punishment for provable misbehaviour.
Smart contract
Code stored and executed on a blockchain with results verified by all nodes.
Soft fork / hard fork
A backwards-compatible rule tightening / an incompatible rule change that requires all nodes to upgrade.
Stablecoin
A token designed to hold a stable value, usually pegged to a currency.
Staking
Locking tokens to take part in proof-of-stake consensus and earn rewards.
Sybil attack
Creating many fake identities to gain disproportionate influence.
Taproot
A 2021 Bitcoin upgrade adding Schnorr signatures and flexible spending conditions.

The threshold a block hash must fall below; smaller targets are harder to meet.
Token
An asset recorded in a smart contract on an existing chain, as opposed to the chain's native coin.
UTXO
Unspent transaction output: a discrete coin that can be spent once, as in Bitcoin.
Validator
A proof-of-stake participant that proposes and attests to blocks.
Wallet
Software or hardware that stores keys and signs transactions. It does not hold coins themselves.
Zero-knowledge proof
A proof that a statement is true that reveals nothing else about the underlying data.
ZK rollup
A rollup that posts a cryptographic validity proof with each batch.
